There should be a waiver. You should have to sign something before you are allowed to love a Seattle team, the way you sign something before they let you on the roller coaster, acknowledging that you understand the risks and that the management is not liable for what happens to your heart.

Nobody reads it, of course. You become a Seattle fan the way most people become anything, which is by accident, as a child, before you know enough to be afraid. By the time you understand the deal you have signed, it is far too late, and you are already the kind of person who can tell a stranger the exact down and distance of a play that ruined a February years ago.

Because that is the harsh reality of diehard fandom in this town, and it deserves to be stated plainly instead of dressed up as romance. Seattle does not collect trophies. Seattle collects case law. We do not have heartbreaks here, not in the casual way other cities use the word. We have a docket, and the files are detailed, and every diehard is qualified to argue any of them in front of a judge.

Start with the famous one, since you are already thinking about it. Second and goal at the one. Twenty-six seconds left in a Super Bowl. The ball in the hands of the possibly best running back of his era, the title sitting right there waiting to be handed off, and the franchise chose, freely, on purpose, to throw it. I will not describe what happened next. You already cried. Everyone who lives here cried at the same word in the same spot.

That is the loud one. The quiet ones are worse, because the quiet ones accumulate, and accumulation is its own kind of cruelty.

The Mariners have never been to a World Series. Not once, in the whole life of the franchise, a sentence that ought to be read sitting down. They once won 116 games, the most a baseball team is permitted to win in a season, and did not reach the Series anyway, which starts to feel less like bad luck and more like a message. We developed some of the best players the sport has seen and watched them leave one at a time, to Texas, to Cincinnati, to anyplace with a deeper checkbook and a thinner sense of loyalty. We watched a man throw a perfect game, twenty-seven up and twenty-seven down, for a lineup that on most nights could not be persuaded to score him a single run, and who pitched a brilliant career almost entirely without ever seeing October. We endured a playoff drought so long it earned its own graphic on the national broadcast, and when it finally broke we got to celebrate for about one round before being shown out in eighteen innings of a game decided by a single run.

This is the inheritance. This is what the colors actually cost. You are not buying a parade. You are enrolling in a long and expensive course on the nature of hope, taught by its absence, tuition due every season, payable in full, in your chest.

So the honest question is why anyone stays. Why a city this practiced in disappointment keeps showing up, keeps buying in, keeps handing its heart back across the same counter that returned it broken last time.

Here is the part I actually believe. A hard loss is the only thing that ever forces the real question, which is what you were hoping for to begin with. Wins do not ask it. You leave a win happy, you get a sandwich, you examine nothing. A loss of the kind this city specializes in sits you down and asks, without any gentleness, whether your hope was riding on the scoreboard the whole time. And if it was, the scoreboard will eventually break it, because the scoreboard was never built to carry the weight a Seattle fan insists on putting there.

The old diehards have figured this out, the ones who watched a franchise get loaded into a van and still renewed the next season. What they carry is not optimism, which is fragile, and not denial, which is exhausting. It is a longer kind of hope, the kind that can take a second-and-goal to the chest and still walk to the parking garage upright. A short hope lives and dies on the result. A long hope does not. For a Christian, that long hope is not a brave face or a coping mechanism. It has a name and a person attached to it, and it does not rise and fall with the standings, which is the entire reason it can hold what the standings cannot. That is not me changing the subject off of sports. That is me naming the only thing I have found that survives being a fan here.

And there is, for what it is worth, a real beauty in losing well, which this town has had more chances to practice than most. The handshake at midfield. The kid who claps for the other team's best player because no one has yet taught him that he is supposed to be bitter about it. None of it makes the highlight reel. All of it is the moment the game quietly becomes larger than the game.

So that is the fine print, read aloud at last. You will lose more than you win. You will remember the losses with a precision the wins never earn. You will hand your heart back across that counter every single year, knowing.

And you will keep doing it, because somewhere under all the case law you still suspect the losses are trying to teach you the one thing worth learning, which is the difference between a hope the night just broke and a hope the night could not touch.

Sign your life away. We all did. Go Seattle.
