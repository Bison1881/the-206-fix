There should be a waiver. You should have to sign something before you are allowed to love a Seattle team, like before they let you on the roller coaster, acknowledging that you understand the risks and that the management is not liable for what happens to your heart. And, you gotta be a certain height, too. 

Nobody reads it, of course. You become a Seattle fan the way most people become anything, which is by accident, usually as a child or as a football baby, before you know enough to be afraid. By the time you understand the deal you've signed, it's far too late, and you're already the kind of person who can tell a stranger the exact down and distance of a play that ruined a February years ago.

Because that's the harsh reality of diehard fandom in this town, and it deserves to be stated plainly instead of dressed up as romance. Seattle hasn't collected many trophies. With some exceptions (yes, things are not ALL bad), Seattle collects heartache. Moments turn into incident reports, expectations crushed by failures of varying degrees. You see Jermaine Kearse make an impossible catch to keep the game alive. Then, you know.

Ah well, since you're already thinking about it... second and goal at the one. Twenty-six seconds left in a Super Bowl. The ball in the hands of the possibly best running back of his era, the title sitting right there waiting to be handed off for the second straight year, and the franchise chose, freely, on purpose, to throw it. Why? Because someone was chasing the MVP. Or, so I'm told. I won't describe any further. You already cried. Everyone who bleeds action green cried in unison and to this day still won't talk about it.

That's the cruel one. Others cut deeply too, because they accumulate, and accumulation is its own kind of cruelty. See the loop?

On a another cruel note, the Mariners have never been to a World Series. Not once, in the whole life of the franchise, a sentence that ought to be read sitting down. They once won 116 games, the most a baseball team has ever won in a season, and still didn't reach the World Series, which starts to feel less like bad luck and more like a sentence handed down with evil laughter, probably wearing a Yankees hat. We developed some of the best players the sport has seen and watched them leave one at a time, to Texas, to Cincinnati, and so on. We watched a man throw a perfect game, twenty-seven up and twenty-seven down, for a lineup that on most nights could not be persuaded to score him a single run, and who pitched a brilliant career almost entirely without ever seeing October. We endured a playoff drought so long it earned its own graphic on the national broadcast, and when it finally broke we got to celebrate for about one round before being shown out in eighteen innings of a game decided by a single run.

This is the life. This is what the colors actually cost. You're not buying a parade. You're enrolling in a long and exhaustive course on the nature of hope, taught by its absence, tuition due every season, payable in full, in your soul.

So the honest question is why anyone stays. Why a city this practiced in disappointment and heartache keeps showing up, keeps buying in, keeps handing its heart back across the same counter that returned it broken last time.

And there is, for what it's worth, a real beauty in losing well, which this town has had more chances to practice than most. The handshake at midfield. The kid who claps for the other team's best player. The complimentary words of a somber press conference. None of it makes the highlight reel. You just keep on keeping on, hoping against hope that one day, things will change and we'll get to experience more success than failure. Its happened a few times. It can happen many more.

So that's the fine print, read aloud at last. You will lose more than you win. You will remember the losses far more than the wins. You will hand your heart back across that counter every single year, knowing. And you will keep doing it, because somewhere under all the examination and speculation, the losses are just as necessary as the wins. Seattle sports culture is an enduring one. You'll learn about hope and you'll learn about disappointment through experience.

Sign your life away. We all did. Go Seattle.
