For most franchises, "wait till next year" is a phrase. For the Mariners it is closer to a unit of measurement, like the meter or the teaspoon. You could calibrate scientific instruments with it. One Mariners Next Year equals roughly twelve months of being told the corner is about to be turned, followed by the corner staying exactly where it has always been, untouched, possibly bolted to the floor.

I have lived through enough of these to qualify as a longitudinal study. I have been promised a corner-turn by men in pleated khakis at six different press conferences. I have watched a new analytics philosophy get introduced with a slide deck. I have believed, out loud, in front of other people, that signing a 34-year-old reliever was the missing piece. I am not a smart man. I am a Mariners fan, which is a related but distinct condition.

So understand the courage it takes for me to report the following: this team is actually good.

Not good in the way the marketing department means it. Good in the way the math means it. The position-player pipeline, which spent a decade functioning as the franchise's open wound, has started producing players who can hit major league pitching on purpose and repeatedly. The bullpen is reliably above average, which for a Mariners bullpen is roughly as plausible as a unicorn filing a tax return. The rotation has front-end stuff. None of those sentences could have been typed five years ago without a doctor's note.

Which is exactly the problem. A Mariners fan does not know how to hold a functioning team. We have the grip strength for suffering. We are not trained for this. Hand us a credible contender and we stand there like a dog that finally caught the car, holding hope we have no idea what to do with, waiting for the part where it goes wrong, because the part where it goes wrong is the only part we have ever been issued.

And there is still one place it can go wrong, and it is the same place it always goes wrong, and it has nothing to do with prospects.

It is ownership. It is the checkbook. It is the franchise's longstanding and deeply held religious belief that Seattle is a "market" the way a folding card table is a "kitchen." Every winter we are read the same scripture. The bit about market size. The bit about payroll discipline. The bit where a player we developed and loved signs somewhere else for money we apparently keep in a different, sadder bank. The front office can build a contender. It cannot make ownership spend like one. That requires a will the spreadsheet cannot supply, and the spreadsheet is the only thing around here that has ever consistently shown up.

Here is the fact I keep on a little card in my wallet, in case I ever feel optimism approaching and need to defend myself. This franchise has never been to a World Series. Not once. It once won 116 games, which is the most a team is allowed to win, and did not go. It once watched a man throw a perfect game for a lineup that scored him no runs, which is the most Mariners thing that has ever happened and possibly that ever will.

So when I tell you this is the most credible Mariners team since the early 2000s, hear the love in it and also hear the flinch. The math is working. The infrastructure is in place. What remains is a single word, addressed to ownership, that I will now type in full and then go lie down.

Spend.
